# 30-Day Executive Presence Plan (reference for the executive-interview-coach skill)

This is a sample plan and a bank of frameworks, not a script. Every example below uses placeholders in [brackets]. Never present a placeholder or an example number as a fact about the user: use the user's own scope, results and numbers, or ask for them.

## Leadership tiers and their lenses

| Tier | Primary lens | Core focus | Currency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Manager | Execution and people | Delivering projects, unblocking the team, hiring, 1:1s | Delivery, project success, retention |
| Director | Strategy and portfolio | Multi-team alignment, operating efficiency, resource allocation | Business outcomes, cross-functional trust, leverage |
| VP | Scale and organizational ROI | Strategy tied to revenue, org structure, capital and operating budgets | Scalability, risk, talent pipeline, return on investment |
| Chief officer / executive | Business and vision | Multi-year vision, market position, board and investor confidence | Competitive advantage, enterprise value |

## Translating the template to the user's field

The plan was written for engineering leaders. For any other field keep the structure and swap the content.
- Engineering: platform, latency, reliability, delivery velocity, build versus buy, technical debt as business risk.
- Sales or revenue: pipeline, quota attainment, win rate, deal size, customer concentration, forecast risk.
- Product: roadmap trade-offs, adoption, retention, discovery-to-delivery speed.
- Operations or supply chain: cost per unit, service levels, cycle time, risk exposure.
- Finance: margin, cash, forecast accuracy, capital allocation.
- People or HR: attrition, time to hire, engagement, org design.
- Individual contributor or early manager: use the Manager and Director lenses, and frame scope as influence without authority.
Rule: the structure (bottom line first, trade-offs, business outcome, composure) is universal; the nouns are the user's.

## Intake: Tell Me About Yourself (profile questions)

Ask these before drilling, in one short message, and accept partial answers:
1. Current role, and the tier you are aiming for (and by when, if there is a date).
2. Your field and the scale you operate at (team or org size, budget, customers, revenue, or the closest equivalent).
3. Two or three results you are proudest of, with the real numbers you can defend.
4. The situation you most want to handle better (an interview, board or leadership meetings, stakeholder pushback, incidents, budget conversations).
5. Where you want to practice: typed answers, or spoken answers.
Restate the answers as a short "your profile" line so it can be reused in later sessions.

## Executive Tell Me About Yourself: Anchor, Pillars, Vision (75 to 90 seconds)

A chronological resume is the common mistake. Anchor on scope, leverage and trajectory instead.
1. Anchor (about 15 seconds, 35 to 40 words): who you are at executive scale, your operating scale, your core domain.
2. Pillars (45 to 60 seconds, 100 to 140 words): two or three strategic themes, each combining a strategy move, an organizational move and a business result with a real number.
3. Vision (about 15 seconds, 35 to 40 words): why this scope fits you now.
Example shape: "I lead [scope] in [field]. First, [result with metric]. Second, [org or capability built, with outcome]. Third, [how you work that scales]. What drives me now is [forward scope]."
Check: does every pillar end in a business outcome? Is the scale stated in the first sentence? Is the total under about 225 words?

## Executive phrasing: replace weak habits

| Weak habit | Executive alternative | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "To be honest..." | "Here is what I see..." / "Based on the data..." | Removes the implied dishonesty of everything before it |
| "I think we should..." (when you have a recommendation) | "I recommend [X] because [reason]." | Moves from opinion to a decision with a reason |
| "We don't have enough time or people." | "To deliver [goal] by [date], the trade-offs are [A] or [B]." | Frames a constraint as a choice with ROI |
| "My team built X." | "We unlocked [business value] by [capability]." | Ties output to outcome |
| "We had an outage because a vendor failed." | "We had an availability drop from an upstream dependency. Here is how we insulate against it." | Owns the result, states the fix |
| "I don't know." | "I don't have that exact figure at hand. Here is the baseline, and I'll follow up with the precise number." | Keeps credibility |
Honesty rule: "I think" is fine when you are genuinely uncertain. State the recommendation first, then the confidence level ("high confidence", "about 70 percent sure").

## The 90-second pitch for a decision (Context, Choice, Outcome)

1. Business context and constraint (15 to 20 seconds): what business driver forces the decision.
2. Strategic choice and trade-off (about 30 seconds): what you chose and what you explicitly rejected.
3. Measurable outcome (20 to 30 seconds): effect on cost, speed, revenue, risk or service levels.
Self-audit: take your last three status updates and rewrite each with half the detail and twice the business impact.

## Director lens: portfolio and trade-offs

Filter every answer through three questions: How does investing in one team or project affect the others? Does this move the top business goals? Am I building trust across functions or creating friction?
Blueprint for "How do you handle competing priorities?": (1) say conflict is healthy and expected; (2) ground the discussion in the company's stated goals; (3) present options with cost of delay, not a yes or no; (4) make the other leaders choose the risk with you.
Alignment reframes: "Product keeps changing requirements" becomes "we need a tighter discovery-to-delivery loop." "We need to hire ten people" becomes "to capture [market] by [date], adding capacity in [area] has the highest return."
Practice: schedule 15-minute syncs with non-technical or non-functional stakeholders and ask "What is the single biggest risk to your goals that my area controls?"

## VP lens: scale, return and organization

Questions test fluency in finance, organization and risk.
- Defending a cost increase: show what the increase bought (growth, unit cost, risk reduced) with the user's real numbers. Frame spend as capital versus operating investment.
- Scaling an organization: align teams to business domains, set spans of control and management layers, create a specialist track, and standardize metrics so performance is visible across locations.
Vocabulary: "cloud costs" becomes "cost per unit and margin impact"; "hiring" becomes "talent acquisition velocity and capacity planning"; "quality" becomes "operational resilience and risk exposure"; "re-org" becomes "aligning the organization with strategic goals".

## Bottom line up front (BLUF) delivery

Structure for any answer under pressure: the conclusion or recommendation (10 seconds, 25 to 40 words), the key context or data (30 seconds), the mitigation or action plan (20 seconds). For behavioral questions combine BLUF with Situation, Task, Action, Result and keep it under two minutes (about 300 words).

## Executive vision lens: market, moat and value

Decisions are strategic investments. For build versus buy use Core versus Context: buy or integrate what is commodity; build and own what creates a defensible advantage, proprietary data or differentiation. Evaluate total cost of ownership over several years against speed to market, and vendor lock-in risk. For a competitor threat, state how you stay differentiated and where you will adopt commodity advances to lower cost.

## High-stakes response protocol

1. Pause two seconds: composure instead of a defensive reaction.
2. Reframe and validate: name the underlying business risk in the question without accepting a false premise.
3. State the action: ownership, the cause, and the system-level prevention, without blame.
Defensive habits to replace: "That wasn't our fault, scope changed late" becomes "our priority is tighter alignment between discovery and delivery so we do not carry execution risk." "We are doing our best with legacy systems" becomes "we are reducing technical debt as a risk-mitigation priority."
In typed practice you cannot be timed on the pause: judge the written answer on the first sentence (reframe) and the last (action).

## Executive audit checklist

- First 15 seconds: did the answer state the outcome or recommendation before the detail?
- Weak phrases: did it avoid "to be honest", filler hedges, and "we didn't have the time or people"?
- Strategic phrasing: did it use "here is what I see", "the priority is", "the trade-offs are"?
- Metric connection: did it tie the work to revenue, cost, leverage or risk, using numbers the user can defend?
- Perspective: was it answered from the right tier (Director portfolio and trade-offs, VP scale and return and organization, executive vision and value)?
- Length: within the target (BLUF about 40 words; Tell Me About Yourself under about 225 words).

## The 30-day sequence

- Week 1, days 1 to 3: executive Tell Me About Yourself. Days 4 to 5: phrasing and the replacement cheat sheet. Days 6 to 7: the 90-second decision pitch and the status-update rewrite.
- Week 2, days 8 to 10: portfolio and trade-offs. Days 11 to 14: managing up and cross-functional alignment.
- Week 3, days 15 to 18: VP-level financial and organizational questions. Days 19 to 21: BLUF delivery with STAR.
- Week 4, days 22 to 25: vision, market and build-versus-buy. Days 26 to 28: stress-testing and high-stakes questions. Days 29 to 30: full mock panel and the audit checklist.
Adapt the pace to the user: skip a day-range they already do well, repeat one they fail the audit on, and stretch the plan if they can only practice a few times a week.

## Sample scenarios to draw from (adapt the nouns to the user's field)

- Resource constraint: a leader above you wants a new initiative next quarter while your team is at full capacity. Present trade-offs, not a complaint.
- Incident: a short outage or miss hit customers at a bad time. Explain it in an executive meeting without defensiveness or low-level detail.
- Competing demands: one stakeholder wants a custom commitment for two large accounts while a core, broad-impact initiative would slip. Resolve it with options and cost of delay.
- Margin pressure: choose between cutting cost by moving work to lower-cost locations or slowing new work for two quarters to restructure. Recommend one, with BLUF.
- Board challenge: a competitor claims the same capability at a fraction of the cost. State your strategic position.
- Impasse: a legacy cost problem and a high-visibility launch compete for the same capacity. Give a precise recommendation to the leadership team.
